I opened my first real brokerage account at thirty. Not nineteen, not twenty-five — thirty. If you read enough personal finance content, that number is supposed to feel like a confession. Compounding rewards an early start, and I didn't have one. For a long time that bothered me more than it should have.

What changed my relationship with the lateness wasn't a spreadsheet. It was accepting that the target mattered more than the start date. My target is concrete: a 1M DKK portfolio, built specifically so that spending part of every year living and working from Japan stops being a wish and becomes a line item I can actually plan around. Once the goal had a number and a reason attached to it, thirty stopped feeling like a deadline I'd missed and started feeling like the day the plan actually began.

## Why the number has to be concrete

Vague financial goals are easy to abandon the first time the market has a bad quarter. "I'd like to be comfortable someday" has no resistance to procrastination. "1M DKK, invested, so I can spend three months a year in Japan without touching my income" is a number I can check against a brokerage statement every month. It's specific enough to make trade-offs obvious: this dinner out versus that contribution, this year's vacation budget versus next year's flight to Osaka.

The number also forces honesty about time horizon. I'm not trying to hit 1M DKK next year — that would mean taking on risk I don't actually want. I'm trying to hit it on a decade-plus horizon, through ordinary index-fund contributions, dividend reinvestment, and simply not touching the account when the news cycle gets loud. Boring, on purpose.

## The patience I already had, just not for this

The strange part is that I didn't have to learn patience from scratch when I started investing — I already had it, from an entirely different mat. Years of aikido training had already taught me what showing up for something you can't rush actually feels like: the same technique, refined slightly, week after week, with no dramatic breakthrough moment to point to afterward. Just steadier hands than you had a year ago.

Investing at thirty turned out to be the same shape of practice. There's no single decision that gets you to 1M DKK. There's the decision to start, and then hundreds of small, repeated, unglamorous decisions after it — the same contribution on the same schedule, whether the portfolio is green or red that month. What I was missing wasn't time. It was the willingness to treat a financial goal with the same patience I'd already been applying somewhere else.

## What thirty actually cost me

I won't pretend the late start is free. Compounding is genuinely time-sensitive, and the years between whenever-I-should-have-started and thirty are gone. But the alternative to starting at thirty wasn't starting earlier — it was starting later, or not at all. Every year past this one that I don't start is a year I can't get back either. So this is the post where I'm writing the number down in public: 1M DKK, no fixed deadline, funded by the same unglamorous discipline that gets me back on the mat and back to shipping code every week. I'll check back in as the number moves.
